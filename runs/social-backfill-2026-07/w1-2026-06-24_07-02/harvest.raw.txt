As engineering, product, design, DS, etc. melt into a new kind of role, I was reflecting on what roles might look like in the future. For example, when I look at the Claude Code team I see what I think is five archetypes:

1. Prototyper: comes up with brand new ideas; churns out many ideas, most of which don't ship
2. Builder: quickly turns a prototype/idea into production-grade product/infra
3. Sweeper: cleans up the UI, simplifies the code and system, unships, optimizes performance
4. Grower: takes a product that has been built and iterates on it to improve Product-Market Fit
5. Maintainer: owns a mature system to make it secure, reliable, fast, and efficient as it scales

Many people span across 2 roles, and sometimes 3 roles. I also notice that these roles are not really tied to job function -- eg. across Anthropic, some designers match category 1, some 2, some 3; same for engineers, PM, DS.

A healthy team needs a mix of these, depending on the product:

- A product that is new and pre-PMF needs people that are strong at 1+2+3
- A product that is growing and has found PMF needs 2+3+4 and some 5
- A product that has strong PMF needs 3+4+5 and some 2

Maybe product roles of the future will look more like this, and less like the domain-specific roles of today?
VERBATIM_END

a company open sources a tool, developers adopt it, build workflows around it, write tutorials, recommend it to friends. the tool becomes the default in its category. then the company closes it down.

google did it with gemini CLI. redis did it with their license change. hashicorp did it with terraform. elastic did it with elasticsearch. the playbook is always the same: open source gets you adoption, closed source gets you revenue.

the real lesson isn't "don't use open source tools." open source is still where the best tools live.

the lesson is: build your workflow so the tool is replaceable.

i use everything. i depend on nothing i can't swap out in a day.

the companies will keep doing this. your job is to build like they will.

The hardest part of building a reliable coding agent is not writing the agent. It's knowing whether it's working.

Google published a blog post on June 30 called 'Driving the Agent Quality Flywheel from Your Coding Agent.' The core idea: there's a five-stage improvement loop that can run continuously against your agent's own production traffic.

Stage one, prepare data from existing traces. Stage two, run inference. Stage three, grade with adaptive AutoRaters that score against your defined success criteria. Stage four, analyze failure clusters. Stage five, execute targeted optimizations. Then ship, monitor, and repeat.

The skill being shipped does the first stage automatically: describe your testing goal in plain language, and it converts that into a structured eval the agent can use to verify its own work.

The pattern I keep seeing across the agentic coding category: evals are the differentiation. LangChain's agent evaluation checklist, OpenAI's guide to agent workflow evals, and now Google's AutoRater flywheel all converge on the same problem. You cannot improve what you cannot measure, and measuring agent quality requires infrastructure that most teams haven't built yet.

The companies investing in eval tooling are Braintrust, Langfuse, Evidently, Arize, and HoneyHive. Most are still early-stage. The category is two or three years behind where observability was when Datadog and New Relic were getting started.

What none of this solves yet is the ground truth problem. Every grading system needs a definition of 'correct.' For coding agents, that definition is harder than it looks: the right code, the right approach, the right tests, the right side effects, all of it correct together.

My read: the teams that define eval rubrics well will have a durable advantage over teams that ship faster but can't measure their regression rate.
